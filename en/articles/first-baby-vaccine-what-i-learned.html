Baby's First Vaccines: What to Expect and What I Learned

What usually happens at 2 months, how to prepare, what's normal afterward and when to call the pediatrician. And how I felt, because I hesitated too.

In short

In the US, your baby's first big round of vaccines usually comes at the 2-month checkup. Bring their immunization record, dress them in clothes that open easily at the legs, and, if you can, nurse, bottle-feed or hold them skin to skin during the shots. It's normal for them to be fussy, to have a sore, red spot or a low fever for a day or two afterward. If your baby is under 3 months old and has a temperature of 100.4 °F (38 °C) or higher, they need to be seen urgently: call your pediatrician right away or go to the ER.

I remember perfectly the first time I took my baby in for vaccines. They were two months old and still looked tiny to me. I watched them in the exam room while we waited and thought: "Is this really necessary so soon?" "What if something happens?"

No one talks about the knot in your stomach when you know your baby is going to cry and you've said yes anyway. But no one talks about how calm you feel driving home afterward, either. Here's what I wish I had known before that appointment.

Which vaccines usually come at 2 months

Vaccines work like training for the immune system: the body learns to recognize viruses and bacteria without getting the disease. They start so early because some of these infections, like whooping cough, are most dangerous in the first months of life.

Under the AAP schedule, the 2-month visit usually includes:

  • DTaP, which protects against diphtheria, tetanus and pertussis (whooping cough).
  • Hib, which protects against Haemophilus influenzae type b.
  • Polio (IPV).
  • Pneumococcal (PCV), which protects against pneumococcal disease.
  • Rotavirus, given as drops by mouth.
  • Hepatitis B. Most babies get the first dose soon after birth, and the second is due between 1 and 2 months, so it may be given at this visit.

Several of these come combined in one shot, so your baby gets fewer injections than the list suggests. Schedules change from time to time, and in 2026 the CDC published a schedule that differs from the AAP's on some vaccines, so your pediatrician will tell you exactly what is due. You can check the CDC child and adolescent immunization schedule and the AAP recommended immunization schedule. If you live outside the US, check your country's official schedule.

How to prepare for the appointment

  • Bring your baby's immunization record and write down any questions you want to ask.
  • Dress them in clothes that open easily at the legs: at this age the shots go in the thigh.
  • Breast, bottle or skin to skin during the shots. Sucking and being close to you calm them and reduce crying.
  • Ask your pediatrician about fever medicine. Don't give acetaminophen "just in case" before the shots, and don't give it on your own to a baby this young: the dose depends on weight, and a doctor should check any fever first.
  • Plan to stay 15-30 minutes at the clinic after the shots.

The moment of the shot

I won't lie. They cry. And something breaks inside you. But the crying lasts seconds. The hug lasts longer. And the protection lasts for years.

What helped me was holding my baby close, already nursing, and talking softly the whole time. When it was over, within a couple of minutes they were calm again.

Afterward: what's normal

Over the next day or two, it is common to see:

  • Soreness, redness or a small bump where the shot was given. The soreness can last a few days, and a hard lump can take weeks to go away.
  • Fussiness, extra sleepiness or less interest in feeding.
  • A low fever (if your baby is under 3 months old, see the safety box below).

These are usually mild reactions that pass on their own. It helps to offer the breast or bottle more often, not to overdress them, and to lay a clean, warm, damp washcloth on the spot (never ice). Whether or not your baby has a fever says nothing about how well the vaccine protects: it protects the same either way.

Serious effects are very rare, and vaccines go through strict testing before they are approved and while they are in use. In the US, the schedule is reviewed by public health authorities and medical societies such as the American Academy of Pediatrics.

Pediatric safety: when to call the pediatrician

Call 911 or go to the ER if:

  • Your baby has trouble breathing, or their face or lips swell, or hives appear on their body in the minutes or hours after the shots.
  • Your baby has a seizure, or is very pale, limp or not responding as usual.
  • Your baby is under 3 months old and has a temperature of 100.4 °F (38 °C) or higher: they need to be seen by a doctor urgently, the same day. Call your pediatrician right away or go to the ER.

Call your pediatrician if:

  • The temperature is 104 °F (40 °C) or higher at any age.
  • Your baby cries inconsolably for 3 hours or more.
  • After the rotavirus vaccine, your baby has severe crying in bouts, repeated vomiting or blood in the stool: call right away.
  • The fever lasts more than 48 hours, or the spot where the shot was given is very swollen, growing or not improving after a few days.
  • Something doesn't feel right to you, even if it isn't on this list.

What helped me decide

It helped me to understand that many of the diseases we barely see today did not disappear by chance. Polio did not stop paralyzing children for no reason. It disappeared from our daily lives because, for years, families vaccinated. Not seeing a disease does not mean it no longer exists: it means prevention is working.

I also came to understand that vaccinating doesn't protect only your baby. It protects newborns who are still too young to be vaccinated and people who can't receive certain vaccines for medical reasons.

And I had doubts, of course. The best thing I did was talk them through with my pediatrician, ask without fear and listen to evidence-based answers. It's not about never questioning. It's about questioning well.

Today I don't remember which day my baby had a fever. I do remember the relief of knowing they were protected. If you are reading this before your baby's first vaccines: it's normal that it hurts you more than it hurts them, it's normal to doubt, and it's normal to feel afterward that you did the right thing.

Frequently asked questions

When is a baby's first vaccine?

Under the AAP schedule, babies get their first vaccine, hepatitis B, in the first day or so after birth. The next big round of vaccines is at the 2-month checkup. During RSV season, babies under 8 months may also get an RSV antibody shot (nirsevimab), which is not a vaccine, unless their mother received the RSV vaccine during pregnancy. Ask your pediatrician what applies to your baby.

How many shots will my baby get at 2 months?

Usually 2 to 4 shots in the thighs, depending on which combination vaccines your clinic uses (some combine DTaP, polio, Hib and hepatitis B in a single shot), plus the rotavirus vaccine as drops by mouth. Your pediatrician's office can tell you exactly what they plan to give.

Can I give my baby Tylenol (acetaminophen) before the vaccines?

Don't give it on your own. The AAP advises trying to avoid pain or fever medicine around shots because it may reduce the immune response, and for a baby under 12 weeks, a doctor should check any fever before you give anything. If your pediatrician does recommend acetaminophen, they will tell you the dose for your baby's weight. Ibuprofen (Advil, Motrin) is not for babies under 6 months unless a doctor tells you to use it.

How long does a fever last after vaccines?

It usually starts within 12 to 24 hours and lasts 1 to 2 days. If it lasts more than 48 hours, call your pediatrician. If your baby is under 3 months old and reaches 100.4 °F (38 °C), they need an urgent medical evaluation: call your pediatrician right away or go to the ER.

Is it normal for my baby to sleep a lot after vaccines?

Yes, it's common for them to be sleepier or fussier that day. Offer the breast or bottle whenever they ask. If they are so limp that they won't wake up to eat, or they aren't responding as usual, call 911 or go to the ER.
