Nutritional foundations at 9 months

  • Estimated daily energy: around 650-800 kcal/day. WHO estimates average total needs of about 686 kcal/day at 9-11 months, with complementary foods covering about 300 kcal of that for a breastfed baby. Individual needs vary with weight, sex and appetite (EFSA).
  • Meals: WHO suggests 3-4 meals plus 1-2 snacks from 9 months on, alongside milk. This menu keeps a 5-meal pattern: breakfast is a milk feed, followed by a morning snack, lunch, an afternoon snack and dinner, plus 2 more milk feeds through the day. Add solids at breakfast whenever your baby is ready.
  • Approximate macro distribution: in this menu, carbs 44-51%, fat 36-43%, protein 11-15% of energy. Single days vary; the week as a whole is what counts.
  • Milk: breast milk or formula usually still covers around half of the energy: often about 500-600 ml/day. This menu counts 540 ml.
  • Textures: thick purees, mashed foods with soft lumps, or adapted baby-led weaning depending on development, plus soft finger foods. Offer small sips of water from an open cup with meals.
  • Iron: include an iron-rich food most days: meat, fish, egg, lentils, beans, chickpeas or tofu, with vitamin C-rich fruit or vegetables on the side.
  • How the numbers work: figures use standard infant formula values (about 67 kcal per 100 ml); breast milk is similar, so the calorie totals hold either way; protein is a little lower with breast milk. Foods are plain, cooked and unsalted, measured in grams (ml for milk and olive oil), with values from USDA FoodData Central rounded to the nearest 5 kcal and whole grams. Treat them as estimates.

From 10 to 12 months: what changes

This menu stays a good starting point until your baby's first birthday. Here is what usually changes over these months:

  • 1Textures: from mashed foods to soft pieces your baby can pick up with their fingers.
  • 2Family food: the same meals you eat at home, with no added salt or sugar and in safe shapes.
  • 3Rhythm: three main meals plus one or two snacks; milk is still part of the day.
  • 4Portions: they follow your baby's appetite; the energy ranges in this guide remain approximate.

For a full week of meals at each age, see the 10-month, 11-month and 12-month weekly menus.

Safety notes for complementary feeding

Pediatric safety

  • Avoid honey, added sugar, and added salt in the first year.
  • Always supervise meals and avoid high choking-risk shapes.
  • Introduce allergens one by one in safe forms and watch for reactions.
  • If your baby was preterm, has growth concerns, or feeding difficulties, personalize the plan with your pediatrician.
Editorial note: This menu is a practical reference, not a diagnosis or an individual medical prescription.

Frequently asked questions

How many meals a day does a 9-month-old baby eat?

Most 9-month-olds have 5 meals a day: breakfast, a morning snack, lunch, an afternoon snack and dinner, with breast milk or formula still as the foundation. In total that is usually around 650-800 kcal a day, depending on weight and appetite. WHO suggests 3-4 meals plus 1-2 snacks from 9 months.

How much milk does a 9-month-old baby still need?

Milk is still a key source of nutrition at 9 months. Many babies continue around 500-600 ml/day of breast milk or formula, adjusted to appetite and pediatric advice.

Can I use this menu for a 10- or 11-month-old?

Yes, as a starting point. Gradually move on to more texture (soft pieces and finger foods) and bring meals closer to what the family eats, with no added salt or sugar. Portions follow your baby's appetite.

Can this 9-month menu be used with baby-led weaning?

Yes. The same foods can be offered in BLW-safe shapes or mashed textures, depending on oral skills and supervision.

What menu should I follow after 12 months?

After the first birthday, babies eat more and more like the rest of the family. To avoid repeating the same meals, use the weekly meal planner for 12-23 months: it creates a fresh 7-day menu with milk and calorie checks.

When should families call their pediatrician about feeding?

Reach out if there are persistent feeding difficulties, poor weight gain concerns, frequent vomiting, signs of dehydration, or suspected allergic reactions.
